# Investigation: which electricity price should the model use for charging a car?

Research only — no code changed, nothing committed. Investigated 2026-07-29 on branch
`worktree-bridge-cse_01ECFeFnu1cGzHZ4G2TgaeNb`.

**Headline: $0.38 and $0.3525 do not conflict. They are two different measures, and both
are correct for what they measure. The roommate is almost certainly right about his own bill.**

Every row matches the residential column to the digit. (CA's genuine *all-sector* average is

**Correction 2: the vintage is April 2026, and it is now the STALER of the two numbers in the
repo.** ElectricChoice's July-2026 page is built on EIA data *through April 2026*. Meanwhile
`ASSUMPTIONS.md:24` (R3, yesterday) cites EIA *Electric Power Monthly* Table 5.6.A at **33.25

date **2026-07-23**; CA residential **33.25 ¢**, US residential **18.44 ¢**).

So: the number the owner wants to standardize on carries an **older** vintage than the number
being retired. Worth knowing before locking it in.

**Verdict on provenance:** `0.3525` = **EIA average revenue per residential kWh, California,
April 2026** — i.e. total residential revenue ÷ total residential kWh, blended across every
California utility and every consumption tier. Sourced 2026-07-27/28 via ElectricChoice's
republication rather than EIA directly.

### (b) What an actual California household pays — utility averages and marginal rates

This is where the roommate lives.

| Utility / schedule | Rate | Date | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PG&E, average bundled residential** | **39.25 ¢/kWh** | March 2026 | Solar.com, *PG&E Electric Rates in 2026* |
| PG&E **E-1** tiered, Tier 1 (baseline) | 33 ¢/kWh | March 2026 | same |

| PG&E **E-TOU-C** peak (4-9pm daily) | 52 ¢/kWh | March 2026 | same |
| **SCE, average residential** | 34.4 ¢/kWh (33.2 ¢ w/ Climate Credit) | 2026-06-01 | SCE Rates Advisory |
| **SDG&E, average bundled residential** | **46.4 ¢/kWh** | April 2026 | SDG&E *Bundled Electric Rate Change Alert*, April 2026 |

Two structural facts explain why every IOU number above sits at or above the 35.25 ¢ state average:

1. **Publicly owned utilities drag the state average down.** POUs (LADWP, SMUD, Imperial
   Irrigation, ~40 others) serve **24% of California retail electricity**; IOUs serve 68%
   (CA DOJ / CEC). POU residential rates run **>50% below** the three big IOUs (Inside Climate
   News, 2025-11-05, on CPUC/CEC data). The EIA state average is a customer-weighted blend of
   both, so no IOU customer pays it.
2. **Tiering and TOU mean the average is not the marginal rate.** A PG&E E-1 customer whose
   household already exceeds baseline pays **41 ¢** for the next kWh, not their own ~39 ¢
   average and certainly not the state's 35.25 ¢.

means per-kWh rates are no longer directly comparable to pre-2026 figures.

## 3. Are $0.38 and $0.3525 in conflict?

**No. They measure different things and both are true simultaneously.**

- **$0.3525** = *EIA average revenue per residential kWh, California, April 2026.* A single
  statewide ratio, blending PG&E/SCE/SDG&E with cheap POUs, and blending baseline-tier kWh with
  above-baseline kWh.
- **$0.38** = an *effective household bill rate*, i.e. what one person's bill ÷ their kWh comes
  to. For a PG&E customer this is not merely plausible, it is the expected answer: PG&E's own
  average bundled residential rate is **39.25 ¢** and its E-1 Tier 2 marginal rate is **41 ¢**.
  A roommate reporting "I pay .38" is reporting a number that sits squarely inside PG&E's
  published range.

The gap is fully accounted for by two known effects, both pushing the same direction:

```
CA state average residential (EIA, Apr 2026)              35.25 ¢
  + remove the 24% POU customers who pay >50% less    →  ~39    ¢  (IOU-only average)
  + move from average consumption to marginal tier    →   41    ¢  (PG&E E-1 Tier 2)
```

The roommate's 0.38 lands between those two steps. **Reconciled, not dismissed.** Neither number
needs "fixing," and averaging them would produce a figure that is not any real measure of
anything.

## 4. Recommendation

### 4a. Primary recommendation (low risk, do this)

**Keep `region.ts`'s CA value and standardize on it, as the owner decided — but fix its label
and record what it is.** Specifically:

1. **Do not change 0.3525 to 0.38.** 0.38 is one PG&E household's effective rate. Dropping it
   into a 51-row table whose other 50 rows are EIA state-average residential would break the
   table's single-methodology guarantee, and — see 4c — it would silently double-count against
   `dcfc_elec_mult_ev`.
2. **Correct the "all-sector" mislabel** in `region.ts:11-13`, the `SOURCES` / `SOURCES_DC`
   strings, and `ASSUMPTIONS.md` §G line 112. The values are **residential**. This is a
   documentation bug, not a data bug, but it is the kind that gets copied forward.
3. **Record the real vintage**: EIA data *through April 2026*, republished by ElectricChoice
   (page updated 2026-07-14) — not "~2026-07" as §G currently implies. Note explicitly that
   newer EIA data exists (May 2026 = 33.25 ¢, EPM Table 5.6.A, released 2026-07-23) and that the
   region table is therefore one EIA month behind `opencawr_data.json`'s R3 citation.
4. **Prefer a trailing-12-month average over any single month** on the next refresh. April 35.25
   vs May 33.25 is a 6% swing in one month; a TCO model projecting over 50k-150k miles should
   not inherit that noise.
5. **Add the average-vs-marginal caveat to §G** in one sentence: *this is an average revenue
   rate across all customers and all tiers; a customer of an investor-owned utility charging on
   top of existing baseline usage faces a materially higher marginal rate, and an EV owner on an
   EV-TOU schedule charging overnight faces a materially lower one.* That single sentence is
   what makes the roommate's 0.38 non-alarming to the next reader.

### 4b. Separate flag on the bare default (`opencawr_data.json` = 0.38)

The US residential average is **18.44 ¢/kWh** (EIA, May 2026). If `constants.elec_usd_per_kwh`
is meant as a *national* fallback for an unresolved ZIP, 0.38 is more than double it. If it is
meant as *the owner's own California number*, it is fine and well-chosen. §A currently classifies
it JUDGMENT/USER-SPECIFIC, which reads as the latter — worth one line in §A stating that
explicitly, since a user in Idaho with an unrecognized ZIP currently gets a 3× overcharge on EV
energy.

### 4c. Should the recommendation be powertrain-dependent? **Yes — and it cuts the opposite way
from intuition.**

The correct charging price genuinely differs by powertrain, because the two owner populations
face different rate schedules:

- **EV owner.** Adds ~3,000-4,000 kWh/yr. That is enough load to make switching to an EV-TOU
  schedule clearly worthwhile, and the CA IOUs actively steer EV owners onto EV2-A /
  TOU-D-PRIME / EV-TOU-5. Overnight marginal rate: **~22-26 ¢/kWh**, i.e. **below** the state
  average.
- **PHEV owner.** Adds only ~1,000-1,500 kWh/yr — roughly a third the load, so a third the
  payoff from switching plans, while EV2-A's brutal 54 ¢ 4-9pm peak penalizes any household that
  doesn't also shift its other load. A PHEV owner most plausibly stays on a standard tiered or
  TOU schedule and tops up at their **marginal tier: ~37-41 ¢/kWh** on PG&E, i.e. **above** the
  state average. *(This is a JUDGMENT about owner behaviour, not a sourced fact — flag it as
  such if adopted.)*

**So the correct ordering is: EV pays LESS per kWh than PHEV.** The model currently encodes the
opposite ordering — `dcfc_elec_mult_ev` = 1.09 vs `dcfc_elec_mult_phev` = 1.00 — for a different
and individually valid reason (DC fast charging).

**Avoiding the double-count.** The existing `dcfc_elec_mult_ev` = 1.09 is internally consistent
with a base of 0.3525 and an 85/15 home/DCFC blend:

```
0.85 × 0.3525 + 0.15 × D = 1.09 × 0.3525   →   D = 0.564 $/kWh implied DCFC
```

$0.564 sits at the top of Electrify America's CA band (48-56 ¢) and above EVgo's ~44 ¢ — a
little aggressive, but defensible. **The important consequence: the 1.09 is calibrated against a
0.3525 base. If the base is raised to 0.38 and 1.09 is left alone, the implied DCFC price becomes
$0.61/kWh — above the typical CA range. That is the double-count to avoid.** Raising the base
without re-deriving the multiplier is the specific mistake this investigation exists to prevent.

If the owner wants powertrain-dependent pricing, express it in the **existing constants** rather
than adding new machinery — that is where the leverage already is:

| Constant | Now | Proposed | Derivation |
|---|---|---|---|
| `dcfc_elec_mult_ev` | 1.09 | **~0.80** | `(0.85 × 0.24 EV-TOU off-peak + 0.15 × 0.50 DCFC) / 0.3525 = 0.79` |
| `dcfc_elec_mult_phev` | 1.00 | **~1.10** | `0.39 marginal tier / 0.3525 = 1.11`; no DCFC component (R7 established PHEVs charge at home) |

Two honest caveats on this option:

1. **It renames the constants' meaning** — from "DCFC premium" to "effective charging rate
   relative to the state residential average." That is a real design decision requiring an owner
   brief and an §B rewrite, not a quiet numeric edit. Leaving the names as `dcfc_*` while they
   encode an EV-TOU *discount* would be actively misleading.
2. **It makes EVs look cheaper.** For a cost-comparison tool, understating EV running cost is the
   more damaging error direction, and the ~0.80 multiplier assumes every EV owner is on the right
   rate plan and charges overnight — which is true of most but not all. If adopting, consider
   ~0.90 rather than ~0.80 to hedge, and say so in the ledger. The model already has a
   "no home charger" intake path (§A) for the genuinely-can't-charge-at-home case, so this
   assumption only needs to cover people who *can* charge at home but haven't optimised their
   rate plan.

**If the owner wants the minimum-change answer:** adopt 4a only, leave both multipliers alone,
and log 4c in §E as an OPEN item. The current configuration (0.3525 × 1.09 = 0.384 effective for
EVs) happens to land within a defensible range by accident — the EV-TOU discount and the DCFC
premium roughly cancel. It is right for partly wrong reasons, which is worth documenting even if
nothing changes.

## 5. Other `region.ts` entries — is this systemic?

**Yes, systemic — all 51 rows share the same methodology and therefore the same average-vs-
marginal gap.** Not enumerating all 50; three that matter most:

1. **TX = 0.1699 — the worst case in the table.** Texas is a deregulated retail market where
   customers choose a plan from dozens of retailers. There is no single "Texas rate" the way
   there is a PG&E rate, so the EIA state average is the least representative of any state's
   entry. It also cuts both ways sharply: several Texas retailers market *free-nights* EV plans
   (effectively ~0 ¢ overnight) while default month-to-month plans run well above average. Widest
   spread between the table value and any real customer's marginal cost.

2. **HI = 0.4662 — highest in the table, and understated at the margin.** Hawaiian Electric's
   tiered residential schedule puts above-baseline kWh above the state average, and Hawaii has
   no meaningful cheap EV-TOU overnight product to offset it. The average-vs-marginal error here
   is one-directional (always understates), unlike CA where EV-TOU can more than offset it.

3. **CA, MA, NY, CT — high-price states with large IOU/municipal splits.** Same mechanism as CA:
   a substantial municipal or co-op customer base pulls the state average below what any IOU
   customer actually pays. CA is the one investigated here, but MA (0.2945), NY (0.2945) and CT
   (0.3224) have the same structure.

Two further systemic notes:

- **Whole-table vintage risk.** All 51 rows come from a single 2026-07-27/28 scrape of
  ElectricChoice, which was itself running on EIA data through April 2026. The table therefore
  inherits one compilation site's refresh cadence for all 51 states at once. Pulling EIA Table
  5.6.A directly (it is a published, stable, monthly table) would remove the intermediary and
  make the vintage self-evident. Same fix also resolves the residential/all-sector labelling
  ambiguity at the source.
- **The gas column has the identical two-source architecture** — `region.ts` CA
  `gasUsdPerGal: 5.65` vs `opencawr_data.json`'s $5.455/gal default. Not asked about here, and
  not necessarily wrong (same "region overrides default once ZIP resolves" design), but if the
  owner is standardizing the electricity story it is worth resolving both in one pass rather
  than fielding the same question about gas next week.
